Day One

A preliminary roadmap for Election Security Navigators, election officials and frontier labs. One road, 12 miles, one mile a month. Three cars. What each driver needs, what exists and who builds the rest.

Ready for Tuesday. For discussion.

Tuesday, 6:10 a.m.

What could go wrong. Nothing here needs a nation-state.

  • Monday, 4:52 p.m.

    A deputy clerk gets an email from the e-pollbook vendor. It uses the vendor's name, her name and the date of next week's special election. It asks her to re-verify her login. The domain is one letter off. She clicks.

  • Monday, 9:31 p.m.

    Someone signs in to the vendor portal from out of state and exports the poll worker roster.

  • Tuesday, 5:40 a.m.

    Every poll worker gets a text from "the clerk." Your polling place has moved. Reply YES to confirm.

  • Tuesday, 6:10 a.m.

    The clerk calls the county IT director. He asks if she has called the state. She asks: called who?

The email is the easy part. An open-weight model writes it on a laptop, in the county's voice, with no one watching.1315 The roster is not fiction. In August a vendor incident in Wake County, N.C., exposed poll workers' names and email addresses. The county's own worst case was phishing.1

Who does she call? The answer should be an Election Security Navigator. This page is the drive that puts one there.

This roadmap is about cyber.

Cyber is a crime. Someone breaks in, steals, locks or changes something. Influence operations are a different problem with different responders.

A deepfake robocall is not this navigator's job. A phishing email that ends in a stolen poll worker roster is.

Two reasons the line matters. Election officials have spent years chasing the loudest headline, and it has mostly been about information. And the cyber community is a quieter room. It does not care who you are. It is a safer place to try new approaches.

Nobody is waiting for this.

There is no galvanizing moment. The federal partner that used to carry election officials' attention has stepped back.

  • Federal funding for the EI-ISAC was cut in March 2025.2 The fiscal 2027 budget proposes eliminating CISA's election security program, including the regional election security advisers.3
  • The Center for Internet Security now charges for ISAC membership. About 500 election offices had signed up as of late 2025.4
  • CISA's Sept. 24, 2026, Election Infrastructure Security Plan says many local offices struggle with basic cybersecurity hygiene and timely patching.5
  • Local officials want the gap filled. Broad majorities in the Brennan Center's 2026 survey support someone providing the training, exercises and incident response the federal government used to.8
  • The navigator model works where it exists. Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota and Ohio run programs. In Illinois every county meets its navigator monthly.6 Minnesota runs an in-house navigator team.7

This road closes two gaps. Navigators have nowhere to meet each other. Labs have nowhere to meet them.

Everybody is driving blind.

Each of the three can drive. None can see the road without the other two.

Put all three on the same road and the view clears. The official knows the road. The navigator knows the map. The lab can see what got repaved. The rest of this page is the drive.

Take the wheel.

Pick who is in the convoy. Drive the 12 miles. Drop someone at any milepost and see what happens.

What you get. What you give.

You know what you need to line up. You do not have the time.

  • One page: what to call them for, and who else to call.
  • Nothing to install.
  • You give 15 minutes: your vendors and your IT contact.

In the frontier lab's car

Build in the inbox and the logs.

All of it inside the office's own environment.

  • A triage assistant that reads a suspicious message and says what it is and what to do.
  • A log reader that explains a vendor's export to an office with no security team.
  • A contract reader that pulls incident terms out of vendor agreements.
  • An exposure summary written for a clerk.

Do not build anything that touches tabulation, voter registration or results reporting. Those systems are certified or vendor-controlled.

What open weights change.

An open-weight model is one whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run or change.12 No vendor account. No usage policy. No log anyone else can see.

For a county election office, that changes three things.

The phish is free and in your voice.

An open-weight model's refusals can be removed with modest fine-tuning,13 and general models are easily turned into phishing-focused ones.14 At Black Hat in August, Google's and Accenture's threat teams said criminal and state-aligned groups are using open-weight models to get around the guardrails on commercial ones.15

The model runs inside the attack.

Attackers now run open-weight models on their own infrastructure as part of the attack chain. No API telemetry. No account to suspend.16 In June, Sysdig watched an attacker wire an unauthenticated Ollama server into an automated offensive tool.16 Google's threat group reports adversaries using commercial and open-weight models to turn public disclosures and patch delays into working exploit code.17

Finding holes is getting cheap. Fixing them is not.

Anthropic's Project Glasswing reported more than 10,000 high or critical vulnerabilities in its first month and said the bottleneck is patching, not finding.18 Anthropic has kept that model off the open market and says a comparable model released without safeguards would make exploiting flawed software cheap for almost anyone.18 Analysts put open-weight models four to six months behind the frontier.19

It cuts both ways.

When an autonomous agent system broke into Hugging Face in July and ran more than 17,000 actions, responders tried hosted frontier models first. The guardrails blocked the attack logs. They ran GLM 5.2, an open-weight model, on their own hardware. No attacker data left their environment.10 Their advice: have a capable model vetted and running locally before an incident.11

Cases to put in front of a clerk.

  • Wake County, N.C. August 2026.

    A vendor with read-only access to a county database reported a possible cyberattack. Poll workers' names, email addresses, training records and assignments were exposed. The county suspended the vendor.1

  • Jefferson County, Ky. 2024.

    A ransomware group posted a list of files it claimed to have stolen from the clerk's office, including an election officers spreadsheet and a file called "Alarm Codes.xlsx."20

  • Brennan Center tabletop. January 2026.

    AI-generated emails and texts posing as the state election security office to harvest county logins, plus an audio deepfake of a state official citing a court order to keep polls open.9

  • Check Point. Early 2026.

    In January, about 1,300 new websites were registered with "election" in the name and about 4,010 with "vote." The threats target email, phishing and fundraising, not voting machines.21

  • Hugging Face. July 2026.

    An intrusion run end to end by an autonomous agent system. Investigated with an open-weight model after hosted models refused.10

The threat is cheap, local and unmonitored. The defense has to be cheap, local and present. A person with a phone, a kit and a model that runs in the office.
