Navigators first

Election officials already have people they know and trust when it comes to cybersecurity. In a growing number of states, those people are election security navigators. This is a working plan for strengthening that model, with AI companies as partners in the work.

Ready for Tuesday. For discussion.

The basic idea.

AI companies have tools and technical expertise that could be useful to election offices. They generally don't have a strong understanding of the day-to-day realities of election administration, or established relationships with local election officials.

Rather than trying to build those relationships from scratch, this effort would focus on strengthening the navigator model. The goal is to build a stronger community of navigators, expand access to training and resources and help them use AI-enabled tools to support more election offices. AI companies would be partners in that work, providing tools, expertise and technical support that navigators can put to use.

Navigators aren't there to replace the people responsible for running elections or managing their systems. They're there to help those people do their jobs more effectively. That includes helping election officials understand new technologies, identifying practical applications, translating technical information into useful guidance and connecting offices with the right expertise when needed.

An important distinction: the initial focus isn't on building AI tools for direct adoption by every county election office. It's on helping navigators use AI to better serve those offices. That doesn't preclude election officials from eventually using these tools directly. It starts with the people who already understand the environment, know where the needs are and have relationships with the offices they support.

A way to picture it.

What navigators would get out of this.

There are already navigators working in seven states, but there isn't an established structure for bringing them together. That's an opportunity in itself.

A community of practice

Opportunities for navigators to learn from one another, share resources and work through common challenges.

Access to AI companies and technical expertise

A way to ask questions, get help and provide feedback directly to the people developing these tools.

Tools that help them scale their work

AI could help navigators support more jurisdictions, respond to common questions, develop guidance and make better use of their time.

A stronger professional identity

Greater recognition of the navigator role, along with a network and resources that support the people doing this work.

What AI companies would get out of this.

AI companies would benefit from working with people who understand election administration and have established relationships with election officials. They would also gain a better understanding of the challenges facing local election offices, which could help inform the development of more useful tools. That benefit is relatively straightforward. The emphasis here is on what makes the effort valuable to navigators and the jurisdictions they support.

Where things stand.

  • Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota and Ohio run navigator programs. In Illinois every county meets its navigator monthly.6 Minnesota runs an in-house navigator team.7
  • Local officials want the help. Broad majorities in the Brennan Center's 2026 survey support someone providing the training, exercises and incident response the federal government used to.8
  • The federal help is thinner. Funding for the EI-ISAC was cut in March 2025.2 The fiscal 2027 budget proposes eliminating CISA's election security program.3 The Center for Internet Security now charges for ISAC membership, and about 500 election offices had signed up as of late 2025.4 CISA's Sept. 24, 2026, Election Infrastructure Security Plan says many local offices struggle with basic cybersecurity hygiene and timely patching.5

In the navigator's truck

What training looks like.

Not a traditional training program. There is a lot to build on already: existing vendor relationships, the cybersecurity support navigators already provide, state-specific scenarios and the meetings and professional networks where navigators already spend their time. Add to that the chance for navigators to learn from one another.

  • Deliver the scenario at the state association meeting, where officials already are. Not a slide deck. Let the room decide what to line up.
  • Run it with a few counties. Each leaves with a short checklist.
  • Bring one tool and run it on a real email from someone in the room. Watching it work does more than hearing about it.
  • Draft the vendor map.
  • Start with the counties whose baseline says phishing is what keeps them up at night. They say yes first.

In the election official's car

  • Run the scenario once. Work the checklist.
  • Ask the navigator to run one tool on one of your own emails.
  • Test the number.
  • Know who can see your poll worker roster.

In the AI company's van

Show the parts working.

Needs real examples

Most election officials haven't seen what these tools do. Neither have many of the people advising them. The examples below are placeholders until teams who build these supply real ones.

Build for the three places an attack shows up first: the email inbox, the vendor's access logs and the vendor's contract. Everything runs on the office's own computers. What it could look like:

  • A suspicious email, checked.

    A clerk forwards an email to a tool. Thirty seconds later: the sender's domain is one letter off from the vendor's, the link asks for a login, don't click, call the vendor at the number on the contract. Ready for Tuesday's Message Triage tool does a version of this today.

  • A vendor's log, read.

    After an incident the vendor sends 400 lines of access logs. Nobody in the office reads logs. The model does, and says: three logins from one out-of-state address Monday night, one export of the poll worker roster at 9:31 p.m.

  • Every contract in the state, read overnight.

    Who has to notify whom after an incident, and within how many hours, pulled from every vendor agreement into one table.

  • A scenario for this county, not a template.

    The story at the bottom of this page, rewritten with this county's vendors, calendar and staff size.

  • A model that lives in the office.

    When Hugging Face was attacked in July, hosted models refused to read the attack logs. Responders ran an open-weight model on their own hardware instead.10 A county office needs the same thing, on a computer it owns, vetted before anything happens.

Nothing that touches tabulation, voter registration or results reporting. Those systems are certified or vendor-controlled.

What the garage is for.

A plan about community and training still has to answer a plain question: what is all of this for? One morning in a county of 40,000. The scenario is fiction.

  • Monday, 4:52 p.m.

    A deputy clerk gets an email from the e-pollbook vendor. It uses the vendor's name, her name and the date of next week's special election. It asks her to re-verify her login. The domain is one letter off. She clicks.

  • Monday, 9:31 p.m.

    Someone signs in to the vendor portal from out of state and exports the poll worker roster.

  • Tuesday, 5:40 a.m.

    Every poll worker gets a text from "the clerk." Your polling place has moved. Reply YES to confirm.

  • Tuesday, 6:10 a.m.

    The clerk calls the county IT director. He asks if she has called the state. She asks: called who?

The email is the easy part. An open-weight model writes it on a laptop, in the county's voice, with no one watching.1315 The roster is the damage. It was gone by 9:31 p.m.

Who does she call? If the answer is a navigator she already knows, with a tool that reads the vendor's logs and a line to the people who built the model, the morning goes one way. If the answer is nobody, it goes the other way.

This is about cyber.

Cyber is a crime. Someone breaks in, steals, locks or changes something. It has a victim, an incident and a playbook. Election offices have always had help with this from somewhere else: the state, a vendor, an ISAC, the county IT director. Nobody expects a clerk to be her own security team, and nobody faults her for asking.

Cyber also has one language. A breach means the same thing in every county and to everyone who hears about it. That makes it a safer place to try new approaches, and the right place to start.

What open weights change.

An open-weight model is one whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run or change.12 No vendor account. No usage policy. No log anyone else can see.

For a county election office, that changes three things. This section is the first draft of the write-up the AI company owes at mile six.

Needs review

Before this section goes further, people who do this work should check two things. Is the threat described as it is? And what does an attack built this way actually look like, step by step, so a clerk can picture it?

The phish is free and in your voice.

An open-weight model's refusals can be removed with modest fine-tuning,13 and general models are easily turned into phishing-focused ones.14 At Black Hat in August, Google's and Accenture's threat teams said criminal and state-aligned groups are using open-weight models to get around the guardrails on commercial ones.15

The model runs inside the attack.

Attackers now run open-weight models on their own infrastructure as part of the attack chain. No API telemetry. No account to suspend.16 In June, Sysdig watched an attacker wire an unauthenticated Ollama server into an automated offensive tool.16 Google's threat group reports adversaries using commercial and open-weight models to turn public disclosures and patch delays into working exploit code.17

Finding holes is getting cheap. Fixing them is not.

Anthropic's Project Glasswing reported more than 10,000 high or critical vulnerabilities in its first month and said the bottleneck is patching, not finding.18 Anthropic has kept that model off the open market and says a comparable model released without safeguards would make exploiting flawed software cheap for almost anyone.18 Analysts put open-weight models four to six months behind the frontier.19

It cuts both ways.

When an autonomous agent system broke into Hugging Face in July and ran more than 17,000 actions, responders tried hosted frontier models first. The guardrails blocked the attack logs. They ran GLM 5.2, an open-weight model, on their own hardware. No attacker data left their environment.10 Their advice: have a capable model vetted and running locally before an incident.11

Five cases to put in front of a clerk.

  • Wake County, N.C. August 2026.

    A vendor with read-only access to a county database reported a possible cyberattack. Poll workers' names, email addresses, training records and assignments were exposed. The county suspended the vendor.1

  • Jefferson County, Ky. 2024.

    A ransomware group posted a list of files it claimed to have stolen from the clerk's office, including an election officers spreadsheet and a file called "Alarm Codes.xlsx."20

  • Brennan Center tabletop. January 2026.

    AI-generated emails and texts posing as the state election security office to harvest county logins, plus an audio deepfake of a state official citing a court order to keep polls open.9

  • Check Point. Early 2026.

    In January, about 1,300 new websites were registered with "election" in the name and about 4,010 with "vote." The threats target email, phishing and fundraising, not voting machines.21

  • Hugging Face. July 2026.

    An intrusion run end to end by an autonomous agent system. Investigated with an open-weight model after hosted models refused.10

The threat is cheap, local and unmonitored. The defense has to be cheap, local and present. A person with a phone, a kit and a model that runs in the office.

What this means for the effort.

This should lead with the navigator model rather than a hypothetical scenario or a particular AI application.

The first priority is developing the navigator community and identifying the training, resources and ongoing support that would be most useful. From there, the work can explore how AI companies could help navigators expand their capacity and what types of tools would make the greatest difference in their work.

What would help now.

  • Concrete examples of a tool or capability moving from an AI company, through a navigator, to a local election office. If you have one, send it.
  • A navigator's own list of what they get asked for and can't do.
  • A read on the open weights section from someone who does that work: is the threat described the way it works, and what would an attack built this way look like, step by step?
